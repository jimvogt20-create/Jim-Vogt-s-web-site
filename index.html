Career highlights and accomplishments:

My steadily increasing responsibilities created a management of a broadening P&L. This high-growth environment honed my general management skills and emphasized the importance of making timely decisions and staying highly adaptable amid rapidly changing market conditions and challenges.

Following Nortel's acquisition of Bay Networks, we became part of the new Nortel Networks. I became the founding President of the Small Business Unit, a $1.5B division responsible for voice, data, and IP telephony solutions, driving new growth in SME and SMB marketplaces whilst maintaining continued growth in the edge business in the Enterprise space. The product portfolio included the BayStack, NorStar Key System, NorStar IP PBX, and Business Series product lines, as well as all existing Bay Networks frame-based switching and connectivity product lines. Our organization encompassed full P&L responsibility and 3,000+ employees, including the WW sales force, which managed 60,000+ channel partners and seven R&D and marketing sites throughout North America. Within two years we grew the combined voice and data business in the SMB market segment at an annual rate of 25%, ahead of the market rate of 15% in CY2000.

Post of Nortel Networks, I began a string of start-up ventures. Ingrian Networks was my first as President, Chief Executive Officer and Board Director and also my first private capital raise. We raised $22MM in new capital in an up-round Series B in November of 2001, with Prism Venture Partners, JAFCO, Partech International, and American Express as new investors. We extended and built the management team in Marketing, Sales, Operations, and Service. We secured a customer base of 75+, consisting of Federal Government, Healthcare, Large Financial/Banking, and Fortune 500 enterprises, and grew revenue from $0 to a $12M run rate in just 18 months. The company then pivoted deeper into Data Privacy, raised an additional round, and was ultimately acquired by SafeNet (Thales Group).

My second start-up as President, Chief Executive officer and Board Director, Trapeze Networks, was a restructure and turn-around. The company had grown headcount too fast and burned through a sizable amount of cash before first revenue. The product and technology were solid (controllers and Wi-Fi access points), the market was early, and there was some direct traction in Europe. I rebuilt the entire team in Sales, Marketing, Corp Dev, Engineering, Operations and Support, with the exception of the Founding CTO. We established brand presence and credibility and raised $52.5MM in new capital, tripling the valuation and securing strategic investments from Motorola, Nortel, and Juniper. We moved market share from 0% to 8.5% with the right balance of direct sales and OEM partners, including NEC, Nortel, Enterasys, and 3COM, taking it from $0 to a $100MM run rate, resulting in an acquisition by Belden, Inc., and ultimately Juniper Networks.

Next, at Blue Coat as Senior Vice President and General Manager of the Cloud Business Unit, I saw an opportunity to build a start-up within a public company. The appliance-based business was flat/declining, and there was a need to virtualize the existing security solutions in the cloud. This was a restructure as well, and my team and I built Blue Coat’s cloud security business from the ground up, creating new channels in MSSP/MSP, extending the business beyond enterprises to SME, and creating the cloud-backed infrastructure for WW POPs, new back office processing, and KPI tracking specific to a SaaS model. The new business replaced its declining appliance revenue at a rate of $300MM+ in less than five years. This was a pivotal shift that underpinned its later merger with Symantec and eventual sale to Broadcom.

Zettaset was my next start-up as President, Chief Executive Officer and Board Director, and it was probably the most challenging. The company actually passed through three states and pivoted over six years. We raised a $13MM up round in a booming Hadoop environment and focused on creating an enterprise solution that hardened open source for enterprise customer use cases. Ease of deployment, management visibility, and security were the focus. However, we found that although our pipeline was growing, deal sizes were small and mainly POCs rather than actual deployments. As we pivoted deeper into the data protection side of our solution, we found our IP was infringed upon, and we were in a full-on stall for over two years until a settlement was reached. The company then fully re-focused on a virtualized data protection solution. We rebuilt the products, pivoting to Software-Defined Encryption, and filed 15 new patents. New markets included Software-Defined Data Center, Containers, DevOps, and Cloud Database protection, with new partners HPE, VMware, and Red Hat. We decided to restructure, raised more capital, and ultimately transferred the products and technology to a new entity.

What's your story?

I was born outside of Chicago in Hammond, IN. My father was a C-level Executive in the Telecommunications Industry, having posts at several telecom equipment manufacturers. His upward advancement and eventual relocations took me across several states, growing up in the Midwest, Southeast, Northeast, and West Coast, through ten schools in twelve years, and three high schools. Restarting and establishing myself that many times not only broadened my experiences growing up but also made me very resilient and highly adaptable as a person and as an eventual professional. In contrast to my upbringing, I have been in the Silicon Valley/San Francisco Bay area since graduating with my BSEE. My father encouraged me to pursue both a technical background and a business degree, which led to my MBA. Upon graduation, I joined SynOptics in a matrix, product management role that allowed me to see and learn all aspects of the company and its business functions. Eventual roles broadened my market and product exposure and created broader responsibility from a P&L perspective. SynOptics became Bay Networks; Bay Networks became Nortel Networks, which led me to running a $1.5Bn division. Post of that, I headed several successful and some challenging startups, and I was able to repeatedly fund, grow, and pivot companies and businesses across varying circumstances and market climates. My dad's advice and my good fortune in having had these experiences in my career have made me a very adaptable and results-driven executive. Deep product and technical depth, understanding the right match and sequencing for Go-to-Market, and building strong, right-fitted, trusted teams have built the successful companies I have engaged with, both as a full-time CEO and as a consultant.

What is the one thing that should be taught in school that isn't already?

The importance of teams and building these with the right fit and culture for the business and company. I think business school focuses too much on individual skills, and although there are group projects, they are not the same. Having to build, and in my case, re-build a team while trying to orient and grow the business is a key leadership asset and talent. You cannot teach that in business school, but its importance for your future success should be stressed more. It has been a vital asset for me and a key to the successful outcomes my teams and I have created.

What is your biggest insight on start-ups?

Reviewing all the different companies I have been directly and indirectly involved with, I have often encountered founders who want to do it all right out of the gate. Boil the ocean - they want to investigate and pursue every potential route to market now. What I have advised is to look at the possible routes to market and really think about what it takes, starting from zero, with usually very little and/or seed capital to do it. This is not just about product-market fit, but also in how it will help build successive routes to market and traction beyond first revenue. The goal is sustainable growth through time and predictive investment to sustain and build further value in the business. Startups need to be about creating predictive growth and sustainable value in the long term. That is what interests investors and raises capital.
